# Tax-Free Savings Accounts: the complete beginner guide (South Africa, 2026)

Voice: Darius (ElevenLabs eleven_v4), one take. Target ~8.5–9.5 minutes.
Every [KEY] marks a scene cue for the animation.

[cold_open]
[warmly] Imagine putting away R3,833 a month. In about eleven years you stop… and your money just keeps growing, for the rest of your working life. And SARS never takes a single cent of that growth.

That's not a trick. It's the law. It's called a Tax-Free Savings Account, and many South Africans either don't have one… or aren't using it properly.

In this video, you'll learn exactly how it works, the two mistakes that cost people money, and how to open one, step by step. Let's start from zero.

[ch1_what]
First, what is a Tax-Free Savings Account?

It's not a special type of investment. Think of it as a container. A wrapper. Inside the wrapper, you can hold different things: a simple savings account at your bank, a unit trust, or an ETF that follows the stock market.

The wrapper's job is simple. Whatever your money earns inside it is protected from tax. TFSAs started on 1 March 2015, and today most banks and investment platforms in South Africa offer one.

[ch2_tax]
So which taxes do you avoid?

Normally, your money gets taxed in three ways. Interest is taxed as income, once it's above your yearly exemption. That's R23,800 a year if you're under 65.

Dividends from shares are taxed at 20%. And when an investment grows and you sell it, you can pay capital gains tax on the profit above R50,000 a year.

Inside a TFSA, none of that applies. No income tax on interest. No dividends tax. No capital gains tax. And here's a bonus: TFSA interest doesn't even use up your normal R23,800 interest exemption. You keep that for your other savings.

Let's make that real. Say you have R500,000 in a normal savings account earning 8% a year. That's R40,000 of interest. The first R23,800 is exempt, so R16,200 is taxable. If you pay 26% tax, that's about R4,200 to SARS. Every single year. Inside a TFSA, that bill is zero.

[ch3_limits]
Of course, there's a limit. Actually, there are two.

The yearly limit. From 1 March 2026, you can put in up to R46,000 per tax year. That's about R3,833 a month. When TFSAs started, the limit was R30,000. It went up to R33,000, then R36,000, and now R46,000, the biggest increase so far.

And the lifetime limit: R500,000 per person, for your whole life.

One thing catches people out. Unused space does not carry over. If you only put in R20,000 this tax year, the other R26,000 is gone forever. Next year, you start again at R46,000.

At the full amount, it takes about eleven years to reach the R500,000 lifetime limit.

[ch4_growth]
Now the best part. The limits only count what you put in… not what it grows into.

[curious] Let's look at an example. These numbers are only an illustration, not a promise.

Say you start at 25. You put in R3,833 a month until you reach the R500,000 limit, at about age 36. Then you stop paying in completely, and you just leave it.

If it grows at 5% a year above inflation, after fees, then by 65 you'd have about R2.7 million, in today's money. Only R500,000 of that came from you. About 82% is growth… and every rand of it is tax-free.

Start ten years later, at 35, and you'd end up with about R1.7 million instead. Same contributions. Ten fewer years of growth. [softly] That's why starting early matters so much.

[ch5_trap1]
[serious] But there are two traps. Trap number one: going over the limit.

The limits are for you as a person, not per account. You're allowed to have more than one TFSA, but everything you put into all of them counts together. And your bank can only see its own account, so it can't warn you. Keeping track is your job.

If you go over, SARS charges a 40% penalty on the extra. Here's SARS's own example: you put in R50,000 in one tax year. The limit is R46,000, so you're R4,000 over. Forty percent of R4,000 is a R1,600 penalty.

Even a tiny mistake counts. A debit order of R3,834 a month adds up to R46,008 for the year. That's R8 over the limit.

[ch6_trap2]
Trap number two: taking money out.

You can withdraw from a TFSA at any time. But the space you used is gone. If you take out R10,000 and later put it back, SARS treats that as a new R10,000 contribution. It counts against your yearly limit and your lifetime limit again.

So a TFSA is not your emergency fund. Keep emergency money in a normal savings account, and let your TFSA do the long, slow work.

[ch7_transfer]
What if you find a better provider? You don't have to withdraw. You can transfer.

Ask your new provider to move your TFSA across. A proper transfer is not a new contribution, so it doesn't touch your limits. The rules say it must be done within ten business days of your request.

What you must never do is withdraw the money yourself and deposit it somewhere else. That counts as a brand new contribution.

[ch8_inside]
So what should you put inside your TFSA?

There are two main options. A cash option, like a bank savings or fixed deposit account. It's simple and safe, but the growth is usually slow, and fixed terms can lock your money for up to five years.

Or an investment option, like a unit trust or an ETF. An ETF can follow a whole stock market index, so one purchase spreads your money across many companies at once. The value goes up and down, sometimes a lot, but over long periods it has more room to grow. That's why many people use their TFSA for money they won't need for ten years or more.

One thing you can't do: buy individual company shares directly inside a TFSA. You invest through funds instead.

The rules also protect you. Providers can't charge performance fees on a TFSA, fees must be reasonable, and withdrawals must be paid out within seven business days. But fees still differ between products, so always compare before you choose.

[ch8b_ra]
A common question: should you use a TFSA or a retirement annuity?

They work in opposite ways. A retirement annuity gives you a tax deduction now, when you pay in, but you're taxed when you take it out, and most of your money stays locked until retirement. A TFSA gives you no deduction now, but everything comes out tax-free, and you can reach your money if you really need to.

They're not rivals. Many people use both: the retirement annuity for retirement, and the TFSA for long-term goals and extra freedom.

[ch9_kids]
You can also open a TFSA for your children.

A child's TFSA uses the child's own limits, not yours. So you're using up part of their R500,000 lifetime limit, which can give them a huge head start. Just remember: when money is withdrawn, it has to be paid into an account in the child's name.

[ch10_open]
Ready to open one? Here's how.

Step one: choose a provider. That can be your bank, or an investment platform.

Step two: have your ID ready, and possibly a proof of address, for the normal FICA checks.

Step three: choose what goes inside, cash or investments, and check the fees.

Step four: set up a debit order that stays under the limit, like R3,833 a month.

And watch the deadline. The tax year ends on the last day of February, and some providers stop accepting deposits a few days before that.

[ch11_recap]
Let's recap. A TFSA is a wrapper that protects your money from tax. You can put in R46,000 a year, and R500,000 for life. Growth doesn't count towards the limits. Don't go over the limit, don't treat it like an emergency fund, and if you switch providers, transfer instead of withdrawing.

[warmly] Start small if you have to, but start early. Your future self will thank you.
