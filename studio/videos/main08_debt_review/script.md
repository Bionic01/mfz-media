# Debt review vs debt consolidation vs debt counselling: which one fits your situation (South Africa)

Voice: Sizwe (ElevenLabs eleven_v4, qp5KnBkVXn1iOyKYgIDA), one take per part. Facts: facts.md (researched 19 Nov 2026).
Thabo is an illustration, not a real person.

[cold_open]
Meet Thabo. He's thirty-four, he works in a warehouse in Pretoria, and every month, before he buys a single loaf of bread, more than half of his pay is already gone. A credit card. A store card. Two personal loans. Sixty thousand rand of debt, and it isn't getting smaller.

He's not alone. People who applied for debt counselling this year needed, on average, sixty-four percent of their take-home pay just to repay their debt. And in a TransUnion survey in the middle of this year, thirty-nine percent of South Africans said they expected to miss at least one bill or loan payment.

So Thabo goes looking for help. And he finds three words: debt consolidation, debt counselling, and debt review. They sound the same. They are not. Pick the wrong one, and you can pay thousands of rand more, or lose the protection you needed.

Here's the twist: two of those three words are actually the same thing. By the end of this video, you'll know which one fits your situation, what each one costs, and the traps to avoid. Let's start from zero.

[ch1_words]
First, the three words, one sentence each.

Debt consolidation is a new loan. You borrow one big amount, pay off your other debts, and now you owe one lender instead of four.

Debt counselling is the help you get from a debt counsellor, a person registered with the National Credit Regulator.

And debt review is the legal process that debt counsellor puts you into, under the National Credit Act.

So debt counselling and debt review are two sides of the same thing. The counsellor is the person. Debt review is the process. That leaves really just two roads: a new loan, or a legal plan.

[ch2_consolidation]
Let's start with the new loan. Debt consolidation.

Thabo owes sixty thousand rand across four accounts. A lender offers him one loan of sixty thousand, at twenty-four percent a year. He pays off the card, the store card and both loans. One debit order. One date. It feels like relief.

But look at what he signs. Over three years, that loan costs about two thousand three hundred and fifty rand a month. Over five years, only about one thousand seven hundred and thirty a month. The five-year loan looks cheaper. It isn't. Over five years, Thabo pays back about one hundred and three thousand rand. Over three years, about eighty-five thousand. The lower monthly payment costs him about eighteen thousand eight hundred rand more.

And there are limits. The law caps the interest on a personal loan at the repo rate plus twenty-one percentage points. With the repo rate where it is, that's still well over twenty-five percent a year. If your credit record is already damaged, you may only be offered rates near that cap, or nothing at all.

Then there's the biggest trap. The old accounts are paid off, but they're still open. The credit card has room again. The store card has room again. And a few months later, many people are back where they started, plus one big new loan.

So consolidation can work, but only if three things are true. You can still pay all your debts on time. The new loan is cheaper in total, not just per month. And you close the old accounts.

[ch3_review]
Now the second road. Debt review, through a debt counsellor.

This is for people who already can't keep up. The law has a word for it: over-indebted. It means you can't pay all your debts on time.

Here's how it works. You go to a debt counsellor registered with the National Credit Regulator. You fill in a form, and pay a small application fee. The counsellor looks at your income, your living costs and every debt you have. Then they work out a new plan: lower payments, often with lower interest and more time, that leaves you enough to live on.

Your creditors are told. And once they've been told, they can't take you to court over those debts, as long as you stick to the new plan. A magistrate's court then makes the plan an order.

From then on, you make one payment a month. A payment agency splits it between your creditors. Thabo's four debit orders become one. And because the plan is built around what he can actually afford, there's money left for food, transport and rent.

But debt review has a price, and it's more than money. While you're in it, you can't take on new credit. No new loans, no new cards. Your credit record shows that you're under debt review. And you stay in it until the debts in the plan are paid.

There are fees too, and they're capped. The restructuring fee is your first month's payment, up to eight thousand rand before VAT. Then an after-care fee of up to four hundred and fifty rand a month, before VAT. Legal fees come on top.

When you've paid off the debts in the plan, your debt counsellor gives you a clearance certificate. A home loan can still be running. The credit bureaus must then remove the debt review from your record.

[ch3b_side_by_side]
Let's put the two roads side by side.

Consolidation is a loan from a lender. Debt review is a plan under the National Credit Act.

With consolidation, you need a good enough credit record to be approved. With debt review, you need to be over-indebted.

With consolidation, if you miss a payment, the lender can come after you like any other loan. And if the loan is secured, that can mean your car or your house. With debt review, as long as you pay the plan, your creditors can't take you to court.

With consolidation, you can keep borrowing, and that's exactly the danger. With debt review, you can't borrow at all until you're done.

And the cost. Consolidation costs interest and fees for as long as the loan runs, so a longer loan costs more. Debt review costs the counsellor's fees, which are capped, plus legal fees. But the plan can bring lower interest and smaller payments.

One more thing a debt counsellor can do. If a lender gave you credit you clearly couldn't afford, the counsellor can ask for it to be looked at as reckless lending. If a court agrees, it can change what you owe on that loan.

So neither road is free. The real question is which cost buys you what you need.

[ch4_choose]
So which one fits Thabo?

Ask yourself one question first. Can I still pay every debt, on time, every month?

If the answer is yes, but it's tight, start with a budget, and call your lenders yourself. Ask for a better rate or a payment plan. If you consolidate, compare the total cost, not the monthly cost, and close the old accounts.

If the answer is no, if you're already missing payments, borrowing to pay other debts, or getting letters about legal action, then a new loan usually just moves the problem. That's when debt review was made for you.

Thabo checks. He's borrowing from one loan to pay another. He's behind on his store card. So for Thabo, the answer is debt review. His counsellor's plan gives him one smaller payment a month. It will take longer, and he can't borrow while it runs. But the threat of court action stops, and his debt gets smaller every month, instead of bigger.

And whichever road you take, watch out for three red flags. One: a debt counsellor who isn't on the National Credit Regulator's register. You can check the register yourself, on the NCR website. Two: anyone who asks for big fees upfront, before you've even signed the application. And three: anyone who promises to make your debt disappear, or to remove debt review from your record overnight. That's not how it works.

If something goes wrong with a lender or a counsellor, you can complain to the National Financial Ombud.

[outro_end]
So, let's recap.

Debt consolidation is a new loan. It can help if you can still pay everything, the total cost is lower, and you close the old accounts.

Debt counselling and debt review are the same road. A registered counsellor puts you into a legal plan that protects you from court action, but you can't borrow until it's done.

And if you're not sure which road you're on, ask yourself that one question: can I still pay everything on time?

Check the total cost. Check the register. And never trust a promise that sounds too easy.
