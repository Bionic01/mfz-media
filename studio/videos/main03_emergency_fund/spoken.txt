[serious] Imagine your car breaks down tomorrow, and the repair costs R10,000. Could you pay for it from your savings?

In a survey by JustMoney this year, with more than 5,400 South Africans, fewer than one in five said they could.

That's what an emergency fund fixes. In this video, you'll learn what it is, exactly how big yours should be, where to keep it so it's safe and earns interest, and how to start with just R200 a month. Let's start from zero.

So, what is an emergency fund?

It's money you put aside for one job only: paying for surprises. Not wants. Surprises. It's not savings for a goal, and it's not an investment. Its job is to be there, in cash, on your worst day.

A real emergency is something you didn't plan, that you can't ignore, and that can't wait. Losing your job. A medical bill. The car breaking down, when you need it to get to work. The geyser bursting. A funeral in the family.

What isn't an emergency? A holiday. A new phone. A Black Friday deal. Those are good things to save for too, but in a different pot.

Think of your emergency fund as a spare tyre. You hope you never need it. But when you do, you're very glad it's there.

Why does it matter so much?

Because when a surprise comes and there's no money, people borrow. A loan, a credit card, a store card, or money from a loan shark. And now the surprise costs you interest, every month, long after it's over. And debt has a way of growing. One loan to pay for the surprise, then another loan to pay the first one.

Or people take money from their retirement. Remember the two-pot system? Say you earn R25,000 a month and you take R10,000 from your savings pot. At a 26% tax rate, SARS takes about R2,600 first, and your fund charges a fee on top.

With an emergency fund, you just pay. No interest, no tax, no stress. Then you fill it up again.

So, how big should yours be?

The common rule of thumb is three to six months of your essential expenses. Not your salary. Your essentials. Rent or bond. Food. Transport. Electricity and water. Airtime and data. School fees. And the minimum payments on any debt. To find your number, take three months of bank statements and add up only the things you must pay, even in a bad month.

Let's take Thandi. Her essentials add up to R10,000 a month. So three months is R30,000. Six months is R60,000.

If that sounds impossible, don't worry. Nobody starts there. Build it in three levels.

Level one: a starter fund of R2,000. That covers most small surprises.

Level two: one month of essentials. For Thandi, that's R10,000.

Level three: three to six months. Aim for six if your income goes up and down, like freelance or commission work, or if you support other people. Three can be enough if your job is very stable.

Next, where should you keep it?

Your emergency fund needs three things. It must be safe. It must be easy to reach within a few days. And it should earn some interest, so inflation doesn't eat it. Inflation was 4.5% in September.

The simplest home is a separate savings account at a bank. Not your everyday account, where it's too easy to spend.

Once it grows, you can split it. Keep one month in instant access. Put the rest in a notice account, where you give the bank notice, for example 32 days, and usually earn a bit more. Avoid locking all of it in a fixed deposit, because then you can't get it in an emergency.

The good news: bank deposits are protected. Since April 2024, the Corporation for Deposit Insurance covers up to R100,000 per person at each bank, for free, if a bank fails. That includes savings, notice and fixed deposit accounts.

But here's a detail. A money market account at a bank is a bank deposit, so it's covered. A money market unit trust is an investment, so it isn't. It's usually low risk, but it's not guaranteed.

And interest is good news at tax time too. If you're under 65, the first R23,800 of interest you earn each year is tax-free.

Where shouldn't you keep it? Under the mattress, where it can be stolen and earns nothing. In shares, which can fall just when you need the money. And not in your Tax-Free Savings Account. If you withdraw from a TFSA, you lose that contribution space forever.

Let's see how fast it grows. In this example, we'll say the account pays 6% a year. That's only an illustration; check your own bank's rate.

Thandi starts with R200 a month. After 10 months, she has about R2,045. Level one, done.

Then she pushes it up to R1,000 a month. About 26 months later, she passes R30,000. So in roughly three years, from zero, she has three months of essentials saved.

And her R30,000 earns her about R1,850 a year in interest. That's far below the R23,800 limit, so it's tax-free.

The key is not the amount. It's starting, and never stopping. If Thandi had started a year earlier, she'd be a year closer. That's why today matters.

But where do you find the R200?

First, pay yourself first. Set up a debit order for the day after payday, so the money moves before you can spend it.

Second, look at three months of bank statements. Find the small things that add up: subscriptions you don't use, bank fees, takeaways, airtime bought in small bundles.

Third, save the surprises too. A bonus, a tax refund, a stokvel payout, a birthday gift. Put part of it straight into the fund.

Fourth, when you finish paying off a debt, keep paying that same amount, but now into your emergency fund. You were already living without that money.

And with Black Friday coming, one more idea. Before you buy anything on credit, ask: is my emergency fund there yet?

[serious] Now, the five traps.

Trap one: using it for things that aren't emergencies. Be honest with yourself.

Trap two: keeping it in your main account. If you can see it every day, you'll spend it.

Trap three: chasing high returns. This money is for safety, not growth. Investing comes after.

Trap four: not refilling it. If you use it, rebuild it before you save for anything else.

Trap five: waiting until your debt is paid off. Build your starter R2,000 first, even while you pay debt. Otherwise the next surprise just adds more debt.

Here's how to start, step by step.

Step one. Add up your essential expenses for one month.

Step two. Open a separate savings account. Check the fees, because on a small balance, fees can eat your interest.

Step three. Set up a debit order for the day after payday. Even R200.

Step four. Name the account "Emergency fund", so you remember what it's for.

Step five. Reach R2,000, then one month, then three to six months.

Step six. Check it once a year, or when your rent or expenses change.

Let's recap.

An emergency fund is money for surprises only. Aim for three to six months of essential expenses, but start with R2,000. Keep it in a separate bank account that earns interest, not in shares or your TFSA. Bank deposits are covered up to R100,000 per bank. And start today, with a debit order, even if it's just R200.
