December pay lands early. And then January feels like it lasts forever.

Last festive season, South Africans planned to spend more than two hundred and eighty-nine billion rand, according to a survey by the lender Wonga. And in January, when the debt-help company Debt Rescue asked people how they were doing, almost six in ten said they were worse off after the festive season.

It doesn't have to go that way. So here's the lesson up front: plan January before you spend December. When your December pay lands, pay January first, set a festive limit from what's left, and stay away from the credit traps.

By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how to split your December pay, with real numbers. Let's start from zero.

First, why is January so hard?

Because of the gap. Many people are paid earlier in December, before the holidays. But January's pay comes at the normal time, at the end of the month.

Say your December pay lands on the fifteenth of December, and January's on the twenty-ninth of January. That's forty-five days on one pay. Six and a half weeks, instead of four. Standard Bank has called it the forty-nine-day pay gap: for people normally paid at month-end, it can be as long as forty-nine days.

And January isn't a cheap month. Rent is due on the first. Debit orders don't take a holiday. And in 2027, public schools open on the thirteenth of January, with uniforms, shoes and stationery to buy. In Debt Rescue's survey, more than a third of people said back-to-school costs were their biggest worry in January.

Meanwhile, December is the month when shops sell the most. Brand Journal's analysis of retail sales found that last December, shops sold about forty-five percent more than in an average month earlier in the year.

So the money arrives early, the shops are full, and the bills come later. That's the trap.

So let's split a December pay, step by step.

Meet Lindiwe. She's an illustration. She takes home fourteen thousand rand a month. Her December pay lands on the fifteenth of December, and her next pay on the twenty-ninth of January.

Step one: list everything that has to be paid before the twenty-ninth of January.

Rent on the first of January: four thousand rand. Debit orders, like her funeral cover and her phone: nine hundred rand. Taxi fare for six weeks: two thousand four hundred rand. Groceries for six weeks: four thousand five hundred rand. And back to school for her son: one thousand two hundred rand.

That's thirteen thousand rand.

Step two: pay January first. On the day her pay lands, Lindiwe moves that thirteen thousand rand into a separate savings pocket, away from her card. Most banks let you open one in the app, for free. If she can't see it, she's less likely to spend it.

Step three: her festive budget is what's left. From her pay, that's only one thousand rand. But she's also in a stokvel, and in December she gets her payout: six thousand rand. So her festive budget is seven thousand rand.

Step four: keep a small cushion. She puts one thousand rand aside for surprises, a broken phone, a trip to the clinic. That leaves six thousand rand to enjoy December, with January already paid for.

Now, how do you make six thousand rand go further?

Give every rand a job before you spend it. In Wonga's survey, the average person planned to spend almost two thousand three hundred rand on food and drinks, about one thousand two hundred on travel and transport, and about one thousand one hundred on gifts.

Write your own numbers down, by category. Then spend from one place: a separate card, or cash in envelopes. When the gift envelope is empty, the gift shopping is done.

Talk about gifts early. In the same survey, almost half of people said money was the gift they most wanted. So agree with your family on a limit, or pick names out of a hat, so everyone buys one good gift instead of ten small ones.

Shop with a list, and watch the specials. A special is only a saving if it's something you were going to buy anyway.

And plan the big trip home. Book transport early, and don't carry all your cash with you.

Then there are the traps.

The first is credit. In Wonga's survey, more than one in five people planned to use credit to pay for the festive season. A store card or a buy-now-pay-later plan feels free in December. But in January, the first payment arrives, on top of everything else. Before you swipe, ask: will I still be paying for this at Easter?

The second is the quick loan. If you're short in January, a loan from a lender who isn't registered can cost far more than you think, and some will keep your bank card or your ID. The National Credit Regulator's advice: only borrow from registered credit providers. You can check a lender on its website.

The third is the bonus. If you get a bonus or a thirteenth cheque, don't spend it all in December. It's taxed, so you'll get less than you expect. Use part of it to fill the January gap, or to pay off the debt with the highest interest first. We made a full guide on how your bonus is taxed, and what to do with it.

And the fourth is pressure. The National Credit Regulator says it plainly: don't let social pressure get you into debt. Nobody remembers the price of your Christmas lunch in February. They remember if you couldn't pay them back.

And if it's already too late, and January is going to be tight, act early.

List every debit order, and cancel the ones you don't need. If you can't pay a loan or an account, call the lender before the payment bounces, and ask for a payment plan. Missed payments end up on your credit record.

And if you're over-indebted, which means you can't pay all your debts each month, there's help. A debt counsellor registered with the National Credit Regulator can help you apply for debt review. We made a full guide on how that works.

And here's the best way to beat January next year: start saving for December now.

In Wonga's survey, fewer than one in four people said they saved through the year for the festive season. Be one of them.

Take Lindiwe's six thousand rand. If she puts away five hundred rand a month, from January to December, she has six thousand rand by Christmas, without touching her December pay. That's exactly how her stokvel works. We made a full guide on stokvels, and how to protect your stokvel money.

If you save on your own, make it automatic. Open a separate savings pocket, call it December, and set up a monthly transfer for the day after payday. Money you never see is money you don't spend. And in a savings or money market account, it even earns a little interest along the way.

And the one thousand rand Lindiwe kept as a cushion? If she doesn't need it, she leaves it there. That's the start of an emergency fund. Next time her phone breaks, it doesn't go on a store card. We made a full guide on that too.

So next December, January is already paid for, and so is Christmas.

Let's recap.

December pay comes early, and January's comes late. That can be forty-five days or more on one pay.

So when your December pay lands, list everything due before your next pay. Move that money away first. Your festive budget is only what's left, plus any stokvel payout or bonus. Keep a cushion for surprises. Give every rand a job, in cash or on a separate card. And stay away from store cards, buy now pay later and unregistered lenders.

Plan January before you spend December, and you can enjoy both.
