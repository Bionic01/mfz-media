First, what is a store card?

A store card is a loan from the shop, or from a lender working with the shop. You get a limit, say R8,000. You can buy up to that amount now, and pay it back later, a bit every month.

The law calls this a credit facility. It works like a credit card, but you can usually only use it at that shop, or that group of shops.

Why do shops like them so much? Because with a store card, the sale doesn't end at the till. Every month you take to pay, the lender earns interest and fees. That's the business. And it's why the card is offered to you at the till, right when you're excited about the deal.

And here's the part most people miss. The price on the shelf is not the price you pay. When you pay later, you pay the shelf price, plus the cost of borrowing. That cost has three parts.

Part one: interest.

Interest is the price of borrowing money, as a percentage of what you owe, every year. The National Credit Act sets a maximum for store cards: the Reserve Bank's repo rate plus 14%.

Right now the repo rate is 7.25%. So the maximum is 21.25% a year. To feel that: owe R5,000 for a whole year without paying anything, and the interest alone is more than R1,060. Just for waiting. And because the cap is tied to the repo rate, it moves when the Reserve Bank moves. The next decision is on the 19th of November, eight days before Black Friday.

Part two: the monthly service fee. Many store cards charge you just for having the account. The legal maximum is R69 a month, including VAT. At the maximum, that's R828 a year.

Part three: credit life insurance. It covers what you owe if you die, become disabled, or lose your job. The cap is R4.50 a month for every R1,000 you owe. It can be useful. But it's charged every month on what you owe, so the more you owe, and the longer you owe it, the more you pay.

And if you open a brand new account, there can also be a once-off initiation fee: up to R1,207.50.

Small numbers, one by one. Now watch what they do together.

Back to Thandi.

The TV normally costs R6,250. On Black Friday, it's R5,000. That's 20% off. She "saves" R1,250. She already has a store card, so she puts it on the card, and pays it off over twelve months.

Let's say her card charges the maximum the law allows. Her instalment, with interest, is about R466 a month. Add the R69 service fee, and the credit life insurance, and she pays about R548 a month.

After twelve months, she's paid about R6,570.

That's about R1,570 more than the sale price. Her R1,250 discount is gone, and she's paid more than the full price on the shelf.

And remember that version that costs almost double? Here it is.

The salesperson says: "Make it thirty-six months, and it's only about R271 a month." It sounds cheaper. It isn't.

Over thirty-six months, the interest, the fees and the insurance keep running. Thandi would pay almost R9,750. For a R5,000 TV. Nearly two TVs, and she'd still be paying for it when the next two Black Fridays come round.

Here's why. On thirty-six months, the R69 fee alone adds up to R2,484. That's half the TV again, before a cent of interest.

Let's put them side by side. Cash: R5,000. Twelve months: about R6,570. Thirty-six months: almost R9,750. Same TV. Same shop. Same Black Friday.

Your card may charge less than the maximum, so check your own statement. But the pattern is always the same: the longer you take, the more you pay.

So here are the five traps to watch for this Black Friday. Number five is the one that keeps people paying for years.

Trap one: only looking at the monthly amount. "Only R271 a month" hides the total. Always ask: what will I pay in total?

Trap two: the fake discount. A "was" price isn't always what the item really sold for. Check what it cost a few weeks ago.

Trap three: a higher limit. Around sales, you may be offered more credit. A bigger limit isn't more money. It's more debt you're allowed to take.

Trap four: the insurance you didn't choose. The law says you can use your own policy instead, as long as it gives the same cover. If you already have cover, ask.

Trap five: paying only the minimum. The minimum keeps the account open. It doesn't clear the debt. Interest keeps running on everything you still owe.

Here's how to shop this Black Friday without the trap.

Step one: make a list before the day. Write down what you actually need, and the most you'll spend.

Step two: check the real price. Look at the price a few weeks before the sale, and compare two or three shops.

Step three: pay cash if you can. Even better, start saving now. If Thandi had put away R548 a month for ten months, she'd have R5,480. Enough for the TV in cash, with money to spare, and no interest at all. That's the Black Friday deal nobody advertises.

Step four: if you must use credit, ask for the full cost in writing before you sign: the instalment, the fees, the insurance, and the total.

Step five: choose the shortest term you can afford, and pay more than the minimum every month.

And if there's an interest-free offer, read the terms carefully, and ask what happens if you don't clear it in time. Put a reminder in your phone.

And if your store cards are already more than you can handle, don't add to them. Speak to the lender early, or to a registered debt counsellor. The National Credit Regulator lists them on its website.

Let's recap.

A store card is a loan. What you pay is the price, plus interest, plus fees, plus insurance.

The legal maximums today: 21.25% interest a year, R69 a month in service fees, and R4.50 a month per R1,000 for credit life insurance.

For Thandi, a R5,000 TV became about R6,570 over twelve months, and almost R9,750 over thirty-six.

Thandi's lesson is simple: the real price is what you've paid at the end, not the number on the shelf.

So before you swipe, ask one question: what will this cost me in total?
