[serious] In 2003, a small bank from Stellenbosch had about 25,000 clients. Today it has 26.6 million. That's more than any other bank in South Africa, and about twice as many as the next biggest one.

That bank is Capitec. It started as a small money lender, up against giants. So how did it win?

In this video, you'll get the story in plain words, what it actually did differently, and the lesson for your own pocket: how to work out what your bank really costs you each month, and how to pay less. Let's start from zero.

Our story starts in the year 2000, with a company called PSG, founded by Jannie Mouton.

PSG owned a microlending business. That means small, short loans to ordinary people. It had bought two lenders, SmartFin and FinAid, with 68 small branches between them.

Then PSG decided to turn that business into a bank. Michiel le Roux became the first chief executive. He brought in experienced bankers, including Riaan Stassen. And a banker called Gerrie Fourie joined them too. Many years later, he would run the whole company.

On the first of March 2001, Capitec was founded, in Stellenbosch.

That was a brave move. Lending people money is one thing. Asking them to trust you with their salary, every single month, is something else.

Now, here's the important part. Who was Capitec for?

The big banks already had the wealthier clients. So Capitec went after a different market: lower-income workers, and people who'd never had a bank account at all.

Capitec describes its idea in one sentence: banking should be simple, transparent and affordable.

Simple means one main account, not a menu of twenty. Transparent means you can see what you pay. And affordable means low fees. Today, Capitec's monthly account fee is R7.50.

It sounds obvious. But it was a big bet. When each client pays only a few rand, a bank only makes real money with a huge number of clients.

And the clients came.

From about 25,000 clients in 2003, Capitec grew to 24.1 million by February 2025, and to 26.6 million by August this year. That's more than a thousand times bigger.

Branches grew from 266 to 885.

And profit followed. Capitec's headline earnings, a main measure of profit, were R30 million in 2003. In the year to February 2026, they were R16.8 billion. And in just the six months to August this year, R9.5 billion, up 19%.

The founders stepped back over the years. Gerrie Fourie, the last founder running the company day to day, retired in July 2025, and Graham Lee took over as chief executive.

So how does a bank with low fees make that much money?

First, loans. When a bank lends you money, the interest you pay is the bank's income. Capitec started as a lender, and lending is still a big part of the business. Remember that whenever a bank offers you credit: your loan is their income.

Second, the money you keep in your account. The bank pays you a little interest on it, and lends that same money out at a higher rate. The difference is profit.

Third, fees. Each fee is small, but multiply a few rand by millions of clients, every month, and it adds up fast.

Fourth, new businesses. Capitec now sells cellphone airtime and data through Capitec Connect, which it calls the country's largest mobile virtual network operator. And 16.5 million people use its banking app.

Three things stand out in this story. It served people others overlooked. It kept prices simple. And it pushed hard into the app, so millions of clients can bank without a branch.

Quick note: this isn't a recommendation to bank with Capitec, or with anyone else. The lesson is about you, not about one bank.

Because here's what most people miss. Your bank fees are not just the monthly fee.

Every bank charges in two ways. A fixed monthly fee, and then a price for each thing you do. A debit order. Drawing cash. Paying someone. An instant payment. Many people compare banks by the monthly fee alone, and then lose more on the small things.

Think of a prepaid cellphone. The price per minute looks small, but your bill depends on how much you use. Banking works the same way. The truth is, what you pay depends on how you bank.

Let's take Lerato. We'll use Capitec's 2026 prices, simply because they're published and easy to follow. Your bank's prices will be different.

Lerato's month looks like this. The monthly fee: R7.50. Four debit orders, for insurance, a phone contract and two others, at R3 each: R12. She draws R2,000 cash at an ATM, at R10 per R1,000: R20. She pays friends at the same bank five times, at R1 each: R5. Two normal payments to other banks, at R2 each: R4. And two immediate payments, at R6 each: R12.

Add it up: R60.50 a month. That's R726 a year.

Now Lerato changes two habits. She pays with her card in shops, where card purchases are free, instead of drawing cash. And she pays people with normal payments instead of immediate ones.

Her new total: R32.50 a month, or R390 a year.

Same bank, same life. She saves R336 a year, just by changing how she uses her account.

And remember, those are low fees. If your bank charges more for each transaction, the same habits can cost you a lot more.

[serious] Now, the five fee traps.

Trap one: drawing cash you don't need. Each withdrawal costs money, and cash is easier to spend.

Trap two: paying for speed. An immediate payment costs more than a normal one. Use it only when it really can't wait.

Trap three: debit orders you forgot about. Old gym contracts, insurance you don't use, subscriptions. Each one costs you the payment and a fee.

Trap four: doing in a branch what you could do on the app. At Capitec, for example, paying another Capitec client costs R1 on the app, but R6 in a branch.

Trap five: ignoring small amounts. R3 here and R6 there feels like nothing. But Lerato's small fees came to R726 a year.

Here's how to check your own bank fees, step by step.

Step one. Download your last three bank statements.

Step two. Find every fee. Look for words like fee, charge or admin. Some banks list each fee line by line, and some show a total at the bottom.

Step three. Add them up and divide by three. That's your real monthly bank cost.

Step four. Find your bank's pricing guide on its website. Banks usually update their prices once a year. Capitec's 2026 prices, for example, started on the first of March.

Step five. Look at your three biggest fees, and ask: can I change a habit to avoid them?

Step six. Only then compare banks. Use your own habits, not just the monthly fee.
