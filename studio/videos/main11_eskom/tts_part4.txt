So what does this mean for your money?

First: give electricity its own line in your budget, and give it a raise every year. When your municipality's increase comes in July, or Eskom's in April, add about nine percent to that line, before the price catches you by surprise.

Second: watch your units, not just your rand. Write down how many units five hundred rand buys you this month. Next July, check again. You'll see the increase in black and white.

Third: if your household income is low, ask your municipality whether you qualify for free basic electricity. Right now, it's often fifty units a month. The government's new pricing policy, approved by Cabinet in August 2026, proposes raising it to between two hundred and three hundred units for qualifying households, but that is still a proposal.

And fourth: remember that electricity is now one of the bills that grows fastest. When you negotiate a raise, plan a budget, or think about solar, count on it costing more next year than it does today.

The good news? Every unit you don't use is a unit you don't pay for, at next year's price too. In a future guide, we'll go through how prepaid tariffs work, and how to get more units for your money.

Let's recap.

Eskom didn't build in time, load shedding began in 2007, and two giant power stations went hundreds of billions over budget. The debt grew so big that the government stepped in with up to two hundred and fifty-four billion rand.

Today, load shedding has mostly stopped, and Eskom is making a profit again. But it's selling less, municipalities owe it more than a hundred and eleven billion rand, and your price is still rising almost nine percent a year.

So give electricity its own line in your budget, with a raise every year.
