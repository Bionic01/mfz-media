Tuesday night, the fifth of December, 2017. Meet Elsie. She's fifty-eight, she lives in Paarl, and she has saved R100,000 over thirty years. All of it sits in one company. The company that owns the Pep store down her road. Steinhoff.

That night, one share costs about R46.

Two days later, it costs R10. Elsie's R100,000 is now worth about R21,900. She didn't spend a cent. She didn't do anything wrong. Remember that number, R21,900. We'll come back to it.

And it wasn't just Elsie. The pension fund for South Africa's government workers owned nine percent of Steinhoff. Teachers, nurses, police officers. On paper, their fund lost about R20 billion.

So how does a company worth almost R200 billion collapse in two days? And how do you make sure one company can never do this to your money?

Here's the answer up front: never let one company hold all your savings. Spread it out. By the end of this video, you'll see exactly why, and the warning signs that were there for years. Let's start from zero.

Steinhoff started as a small furniture business. By 2016, it was run from Stellenbosch, and it was everywhere.

It bought Pep and Ackermans' owner, Pepkor, in 2015. It bought Poundland in Britain. It bought Mattress Firm in America for about 3.8 billion dollars. By 2016, it had six and a half thousand stores, forty brands, in thirty countries.

Big pension funds bought it. Unit trusts bought it. Ordinary people like Elsie bought it, because everyone else had.

To an investor like Elsie, it looked perfect. Profits going up. A famous boss. Shops she could actually walk into. What could go wrong?

But here's the question nobody was asking loudly enough. Where was all that profit really coming from?

Imagine you run a spaza shop. Business is slow. So you "sell" a thousand rand of stock to your cousin's shop next door. You write it down as profit. Your books look great. Next year, to hide the hole, you do it again, only bigger.

But your cousin never pays. And your cousin's shop? You secretly control it.

That, in very simple terms, is what investigators found at Steinhoff. In 2019, the forensic firm PwC published its findings. Over nine years, there were fictitious or irregular deals worth about 6.5 billion euros. That's more than R100 billion.

The deals were with companies that were "made to appear" independent. But they were linked to the same small group of people. Some documents were created after the fact, and backdated.

So the profits looked real. The shops were real. The money? A lot of it wasn't.

Now, you might think a trick that big would be invisible. It wasn't. The warning signs were there for years. And one of them, you can check yourself, in five minutes, for any company.

Warning sign one: the numbers were hard to follow. As far back as 2007, analysts asked why the accounts didn't clearly show where the revenue came from. Steinhoff pushed back.

Warning sign two: deals with insiders. Money moved between Steinhoff and companies linked to its own people.

Warning sign three: the police. In November 2015, German investigators raided Steinhoff's European office over suspected accounting fraud. In August 2017, a German magazine reported that the boss himself was under investigation. The share price dipped, then carried on.

And warning sign four, the one you can check yourself: growth that's too fast to explain. Steinhoff grew by buying company after company, faster than anyone could check the books. If you can't explain how a company makes its money in one sentence, that's your sign to slow down.

Elsie didn't see any of this. Most people didn't. The share price was still high, and a high price feels like proof.

Then came December 2017.

On the evening of the fifth of December, the auditors, Deloitte, wouldn't sign off on the results. The results were postponed. And the CEO, Markus Jooste, resigned.

Wednesday morning, the share fell sixty-one percent, to R17.61. Thursday, another forty-three percent, to R10. About eighty percent gone in two days. Roughly ten billion euros of value, wiped out. Well over R100 billion.

On Thursday, Elsie checks her statement. R21,900. Thirty years of saving, and nearly eighty percent gone in two days.

Who lost? Big funds, and ordinary people. The government workers' fund, about R20 billion. Its pensions weren't cut, because that fund promises a set pension, but the money was gone.

Years later, in 2022, a court approved a settlement of 1.43 billion euros, about R25 billion. More than 43,000 claims came in, worth about 3.2 billion euros. So most people got back only a small part of what they lost.

And in October 2023, the shares of Steinhoff International officially ceased to exist.

What happened to the people at the top? Jooste was banned from running a listed company for twenty years. In March 2024, regulators fined him a record R475 million. He died the next day, before he ever faced a criminal court. Since then, several former executives have been convicted. The former finance boss got an effective five years in prison.

And here's the twist. The shops were fine. Pep is still open. Pepkor is still listed, with more than six and a half thousand stores. In July 2025, what's left of Steinhoff, now called Ibex, sold its whole Pepkor stake for about R27 billion, to pay its debts.

The shops were real. The accounts weren't. And the people who paid were the ones who trusted one company with everything.

So what does this mean for you?

Let's go back to Elsie. She put R100,000 into one company. Two days later, she had about R21,900.

Now imagine she split it across twenty companies. R5,000 each. One of them is Steinhoff. Same crash. Her Steinhoff R5,000 drops to about R1,100. The other nineteen don't care. She still has about R96,100. Even if Steinhoff went to zero, she'd have R95,000.

Same company. Same fraud. A loss of four percent, instead of seventy-eight.

That's diversification. It doesn't stop a fraud. It just makes sure one fraud can't wipe you out.

And the law already does it for your pension. Under Regulation 28, a retirement fund can't put more than fifteen percent of its money into even the biggest single listed company.

You can do it too, with a simple index fund. One index fund can hold the forty biggest companies on the JSE. Steinhoff was less than two percent of a big South African index, so when it crashed, that index lost only about one point four percent. And before you ever buy one company's shares, run the four checks. Can I explain how it makes money in one sentence? Are there deals with insiders? Is it growing faster than anyone can check? Are the results late, or is the auditor unhappy?

One company can fail, even a famous one. Twenty companies, or forty, almost never all fail together.

So, the lesson from Steinhoff: the shops were real, the profits weren't, and one company held everything. Spread your money, and it can't take you down with it.
