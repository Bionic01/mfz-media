Warning sign one: the numbers were hard to follow. As far back as 2007, analysts asked why the accounts didn't clearly show where the revenue came from. Steinhoff pushed back.

Warning sign two: deals with insiders. Money moved between Steinhoff and companies linked to its own people.

Warning sign three: the police. In November 2015, German investigators raided Steinhoff's European office over suspected accounting fraud. In August 2017, a German magazine reported that the boss himself was under investigation. The share price dipped, then carried on.

And warning sign four, the one you can check yourself: growth that's too fast to explain. Steinhoff grew by buying company after company, faster than anyone could check the books. If you can't explain how a company makes its money in one sentence, that's your sign to slow down.

Elsie didn't see any of this. Most people didn't. The share price was still high, and a high price feels like proof.

Then came December 2017.

On the evening of the fifth of December, the auditors, Deloitte, wouldn't sign off on the results. The results were postponed. And the CEO, Markus Jooste, resigned.

Wednesday morning, the share fell sixty-one percent, to 17 rand 61. Thursday, another forty-three percent, to 10 rand. About eighty percent gone in two days. Roughly ten billion euros of value, wiped out. Well over 100 billion rand.

On Thursday, Elsie checks her statement. 21,900 rand. Thirty years of saving, and nearly eighty percent gone in two days.

Who lost? Big funds, and ordinary people. The government workers' fund, about 20 billion rand. Its pensions weren't cut, because that fund promises a set pension, but the money was gone.

Years later, in 2022, a court approved a settlement of 1.43 billion euros, about 25 billion rand. More than 43,000 claims came in, worth about 3.2 billion euros. So most people got back only a small part of what they lost.

And in October 2023, the shares of Steinhoff International officially ceased to exist.

What happened to the people at the top? Jooste was banned from running a listed company for twenty years. In March 2024, regulators fined him a record 475 million rand. He died the next day, before he ever faced a criminal court. Since then, several former executives have been convicted. The former finance boss got an effective five years in prison.

And here's the twist. The shops were fine. Pep is still open. Pepkor is still listed, with more than six and a half thousand stores. In July 2025, what's left of Steinhoff, now called Ibex, sold its whole Pepkor stake for about 27 billion rand, to pay its debts.

The shops were real. The accounts weren't. And the people who paid were the ones who trusted one company with everything.
