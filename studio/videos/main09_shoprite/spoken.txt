It's month-end in a small town in the North West. Meet Lerato. She gets paid on Friday, and on Saturday morning she does all her money jobs in one place.

First, the counter by the door. She sends five hundred rand to her mother in the next town. She buys electricity, and airtime. Then she fills a trolley: maize meal, oil, bread, chicken. One queue. One shop. Shoprite.

Lerato is not unusual. In its last financial year, the Shoprite group sold almost two hundred and seventy-one billion rand of goods. That's about five billion rand, every single week.

But here's the strange part. Of every hundred rand Lerato spends, Shoprite keeps only about six rand as trading profit. Six rand.

So how does a business that keeps so little of each sale end up worth close to two hundred billion rand? And why is there a Shoprite, a Checkers or a Usave in almost every town?

Here's the lesson up front: a small fee is not small when the amount is small. That counter by the door is one of the cleverest parts of Shoprite's business, and by the end of this video, you'll know how to work out what it really costs you. Let's start from zero.

The story starts in Cape Town. The first Shoprite opened in Wynberg in 1966.

In 1979, a group led by Whitey Basson, backed by the businessman Christo Wiese, bought the business. It had eight stores. The price: one million rand.

Seven years later, in 1986, Shoprite listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange. It was worth about twenty-nine million rand.

Then came the big moves. In 1991, Shoprite bought Checkers, for fifty-five million rand. In 1994, it opened its first store in Soweto. And at the end of 1997, it bought OK Bazaars, a famous old chain, for one rand. Yes. One rand.

Today, the group has three thousand seven hundred and ten stores, and about one hundred and seventy-four thousand people work for it. That one-million-rand business is now worth close to two hundred billion.

So what is the machine behind it?

Here's how a supermarket makes money. It's simple, but it's hard to do.

Take Lerato's hundred rand. Most of it, Shoprite pays straight back out: to the farmers and factories that made the food, to the cashiers and the drivers, and for rent, electricity and fuel. What's left over, before interest and tax, is the trading profit. Last year, that was sixteen point two billion rand, on two hundred and seventy point eight billion rand of sales. A margin of just under six percent.

So Shoprite doesn't get rich from one trolley. It gets rich from millions of trolleys. About six rand in every hundred, on five billion rand of sales a week, adds up fast.

And to keep that six rand, it controls the whole road the food travels. Its own warehouses. Its own trucks. Its own stores. Its own brands on the shelves. Every rand it saves along the way can go into lower prices, and lower prices bring in more trolleys.

There's one more trick. Lerato pays at the till today. But big retailers usually pay their suppliers later, often weeks later. In between, that money sits with the retailer. With five billion rand coming in every week, that's a lot of cash working for the business. Last year, Shoprite's operations generated twenty-seven point six billion rand in cash.

Now, why is it in every town?

Shoprite went where the people were. It started with ordinary shoppers, not the rich. In the nineties, it moved into the townships. Then, in 2003, it opened its first Usave.

A Usave is small, a few hundred square metres. Some are built from shipping containers. It sells a short list of basics: more than four hundred and twenty products under ten rand, even a one-rand packet of biscuits. In August 2025, the five hundredth Usave opened, in Hopefield in the Western Cape. As Shoprite's boss put it, Usave was designed to go where others wouldn't.

And the group keeps two kinds of shop for two kinds of shopper. Shoprite and Usave, for people who watch every rand. Checkers, for the suburbs, with Checkers Sixty60, its delivery app, which sold twenty-five point five billion rand last year.

In that one year, the group opened more than two hundred and sixty new supermarkets in South Africa.

But everywhere has its limits. Over the last few years, Shoprite pulled out of Nigeria, Uganda and Madagascar, and then Ghana and Malawi. Today, it sticks to South Africa and its neighbours, where its trucks and warehouses can reach.

Now, back to that counter by the door, the one Lerato went to first. Shoprite calls it the Money Market counter.

There, you can send money to someone in another town, who collects it at a Shoprite till. You can pay bills, buy electricity and airtime, and send money abroad with Shoprite Send. And with the Money Market Account, an account you open on your phone, more than four million customers can get paid, send and spend without visiting a bank branch. Grant recipients can even have their social grant paid into it.

Why would a supermarket do all this? Because of what happens after the counter. Lerato came in to send money. Then she bought her groceries. Every person who walks in to send, collect or pay, walks past the bread and the milk.

And the Xtra Savings card that gives her discounts? Loyalty cards also show a retailer what people buy. Last year, thirty-six and a half million customers used Xtra Savings, and Shoprite says it gave back eighteen point three billion rand in savings at the till.

So what does this mean for your money? Let's work out what these services really cost.

Sending money from one Shoprite counter to another costs a flat nine rand ninety-nine, on top of the money you send. Flat means the same fee, whether you send a little or a lot. So look what happens.

Send two hundred rand, and the fee is about five percent of what you send. Send two thousand rand, and the same fee is only half a percent.

Now say Lerato sends her mother two hundred rand, four times a month. That's four fees, almost forty rand. If she sends eight hundred rand once a month instead, it's one fee, about ten rand. That's about thirty rand a month back in her pocket. Three hundred and sixty rand a year, for the same money sent.

Lesson one: with a flat fee, send less often, and send more each time, if your family can plan for it.

Lesson two: look for the free way first. Shoprite says sending from one Money Market Account to another is free. If you and your family both have one, you skip the counter fee completely. And the same idea works at any bank: compare the fee for each way of sending before you choose.

Lesson three: a loyalty discount only saves you money on things you were going to buy anyway. A deal on something you didn't need is not a saving. It's spending.

So here's Lerato's new plan. She talks to her mother, and they agree: one transfer a month, on payday, instead of four. She helps her mother open a Money Market Account on her phone, so next time, sending costs nothing at all. And at the till, she buys what's on her list, and lets the other specials go. Same shop. Same family. Less money leaking out in fees.

Let's recap.

Shoprite started in 1979 with eight stores and one million rand. It keeps about six rand of every hundred, so it wins on volume: millions of shoppers, in almost every town, with its own warehouses, trucks and brands. And its money counters bring people through the door.

And for you: turn a flat fee into a percentage before you pay it. Look for the free way first. And only count a discount if you needed the thing.
