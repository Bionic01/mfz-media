His employer works out the tax on his salary for the year, with the bonus and without it. The difference is the tax on the bonus.

With the bonus, Sipho's income for the year is two hundred and sixty thousand rand. The first five thousand one hundred rand of the bonus still fits in the eighteen percent step. But the rest, almost fifteen thousand rand, spills into the next step, at twenty-six percent.

Add it up, and the tax on his twenty thousand rand bonus is four thousand seven hundred and ninety-two rand. That's about twenty-four rand in every hundred, more than double his average rate of eleven.

So in December, his payslip shows about six thousand nine hundred rand in tax, instead of the usual two thousand one hundred. It looks like a punishment. But it's just maths: every extra rand you earn is taxed at your top rate, never your average rate.

And the bigger your salary, the bigger the bite. Say someone earns forty thousand rand a month, and gets a forty thousand rand bonus. Their whole bonus lands in the thirty-one percent step. So twelve thousand four hundred rand of it goes to tax, even though their average rate is only about nineteen percent.

Is a thirteenth cheque taxed differently from a performance bonus? No. A thirteenth cheque is usually in your contract, and a performance bonus usually isn't guaranteed. But for tax, both are simply part of your salary.

Some employers spread the tax on a thirteenth cheque over the whole year, so December doesn't hurt as much. Ask your payroll office how yours does it. Either way, the total tax for the year is the same.

And will you get that tax back when you file your return? Usually not. If your employer took off the right amount, the tax was simply correct. You only get money back if too much was taken, or if you have deductions, like contributions to a retirement annuity. More on that in a moment.
