Your R20,000 bonus lands as about R15,200. Where did almost R5,000 go? Your bonus or 13th cheque is taxed at your top (marginal) rate, not your average rate, so it feels like more tax, but it's just how the brackets work. Follow Sipho (an illustration), who earns R20,000 a month, through the 2026/27 SARS tax tables step by step, then the order that works for most people: pay off expensive debt, build an emergency fund, a tax-free savings account, a retirement annuity (which can get you tax back), and spending on purpose. Plus three bonus mistakes to avoid.

Key figures (2027 tax year: 1 March 2026 to 28 February 2027)
- Brackets: 18% up to R245,100; 26% to R383,100; 31% to R530,200; 36% to R695,800; 39% to R887,000; 41% to R1,878,600; 45% above that. Primary rebate R17,820; tax threshold under 65: R99,000.
- Tax-free savings: R46,000 a year from 1 March 2026 (was R36,000); R500,000 lifetime; 40% tax on any excess.
- Retirement contributions: deductible up to 27.5% of income, capped at R430,000 a year from 1 March 2026 (was R350,000).
- Credit and store cards: the legal maximum interest is the repo rate + 14 percentage points a year.

About the example: Sipho is an illustration. R20,000 a month = R240,000 a year: tax R25,380 (18% x R240,000 - R17,820), about R2,115 a month, an average rate of about 10.6%. With a R20,000 bonus (R260,000): tax R30,172, so the bonus is taxed R4,792 (R5,100 at 18% + R14,900 at 26%), about 24%, leaving about R15,208. A R5,000 retirement annuity contribution at a 26% marginal rate cuts tax by R1,300. Second example: R40,000 a month + R40,000 bonus: R12,400 tax on the bonus (all at 31%). Your employer's payroll method may differ; the total tax for the year is the same.
