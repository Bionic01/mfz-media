So how do you protect your stokvel? Five steps.

One: write a constitution, and have every member sign it. It should say how much everyone pays, and by when. What happens if someone falls behind, or wants to leave. Who the chairperson and the treasurer are. And how and when the money is paid out. You don't have to start from scratch: na-SAH-sa has templates.

Two: open a separate stokvel bank account. Most big banks offer one. Never keep the group's money in one member's personal account.

Three: make sure no single person can take money out. Ingram's advice: at least two of three signatories must approve every payment.

Four: keep records everyone can see. Who paid, how much, and when. Read the balance out loud at every meeting. And every few months, let one or two other members check the bank statement against the records.

Five: join na-SAH-sa. It's more than a badge. Under a 2006 Banks Act notice, a stokvel group that holds its members' savings stays outside the banking rules only if it meets certain conditions, including belonging to na-SAH-sa or a similar approved body, and keeping proper accounting records.

Now, where should the money sit while it waits?

If your stokvel pays out every December, the most important things are safety, and being able to get the money when you need it. Ingram suggests something like a fixed deposit or a money market account, so the money earns interest instead of sitting still.

If your group is saving for more than three years, say for property, he suggests looking at low-cost unit trusts or exchange-traded funds. But only if every member understands that the value can go down as well as up, and the constitution says so.

Let's go back to Thandeka's stokvel. Seventy-two thousand rand, in a stokvel account that needs two of three signatures, with a signed constitution, and a balance read out at every meeting. If the treasurer moves away, falls ill, or simply makes a mistake, the money is still safe, and everyone knows exactly where it is.
