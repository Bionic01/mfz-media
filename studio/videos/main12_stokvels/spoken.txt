It's the first Sunday of December. In a church hall in Soweto, twelve women sit around a table. On the table: a notebook, a bank statement, and a year of savings.

Every month, each of them put in five hundred rand. Twelve women, twelve months. That's seventy-two thousand rand. Today, each one goes home with six thousand rand for Christmas.

This is a stokvel. And Thandeka, who runs this one, is not alone. More than eleven million South Africans belong to a stokvel. Together, they save about fifty billion rand a year.

But every year, some stokvels go wrong. The treasurer disappears. The money is gone. Friends stop talking.

So here's the lesson up front: a stokvel can be one of the best savings habits in South Africa, or one of the easiest ways to lose money. The difference comes down to three things: written rules, a bank account that needs more than one signature, and records that everyone can see. By the end of this video, you'll know how to set up all three. Let's start from zero.

So what is a stokvel?

It's a group of people who save together. Everyone puts in a fixed amount, usually every month. And the money is paid out by the group's own rules.

Even the word has a story. According to the Dictionary of South African English, stokvel comes from the English words stock fair. Said with a South African accent, stock fair became stokvel.

There are a few main kinds.

In a rotating stokvel, each month, one member takes the whole pot. With twelve members putting in five hundred rand, one person gets six thousand rand this month, another next month, until everyone has had a turn.

In a savings stokvel, like Thandeka's, the money builds up all year, and everyone is paid out together, usually in December.

In a grocery stokvel, members pool their money and buy food in bulk, often for the festive season.

A burial society helps members pay for funerals.

And an investment stokvel puts the money to work, in property, shares, or a business.

Stokvels are not small. In 2024, the research firm Ipsos called them the human banks of South Africa: more than eight hundred thousand groups, eleven million members, and about fifty billion rand.

And they're still growing. In 2025, the National Stokvel Association of South Africa, NASASA, said young people are joining stokvels in droves. Ipsos found the same: stokvels are attracting younger people, and people with higher incomes too. And special stokvels, saving for weddings, property, holidays or investments, are surging in popularity.

Why do people trust a stokvel more than a bank? Ipsos found a persistent mistrust of banks, driven by fees, slow account opening and rules about when you can take your money out. And for most stokvels, cash is still king.

But the real power of a stokvel isn't the money. It's the people.

When you save alone, it's easy to skip a month. When eleven other people are counting on you, you pay. A stokvel turns saving into a promise you make to people you know. That's why it works, even for people who've never managed to save on their own.

But that same trust is also the biggest risk.

Ask the financial adviser Warren Ingram what goes wrong in stokvels, and he says almost every problem comes down to one thing: nothing was written down.

No rule for what happens if someone stops paying. No rule for who can leave, and when. No rule for how the payout works. So when something goes wrong, people argue, and friendships end.

The second risk is where the money sits. Many stokvels keep the money in one person's own bank account. That feels simple. But that money is now exposed to anything that happens to that one person: theft, death, divorce, or debts they owe. If the treasurer passes away, the stokvel's money can get tied up in their estate.

The third risk is cash. A year of savings kept at home can be stolen in one night. And money in a cupboard earns nothing, while prices keep going up.

And in a rotating stokvel, there's a fourth risk. The person who gets the pot first has had their payout, but still has to keep paying for months. If they stop, the people at the end of the line carry the loss. That's why the order of payouts, and what happens if someone stops paying, must be written down from day one.

So how do you protect your stokvel? Five steps.

One: write a constitution, and have every member sign it. It should say how much everyone pays, and by when. What happens if someone falls behind, or wants to leave. Who the chairperson and the treasurer are. And how and when the money is paid out. You don't have to start from scratch: NASASA has templates.

Two: open a separate stokvel bank account. Most big banks offer one. Never keep the group's money in one member's personal account.

Three: make sure no single person can take money out. Ingram's advice: at least two of three signatories must approve every payment.

Four: keep records everyone can see. Who paid, how much, and when. Read the balance out loud at every meeting. And every few months, let one or two other members check the bank statement against the records.

Five: join NASASA. It's more than a badge. Under a 2006 Banks Act notice, a stokvel group that holds its members' savings stays outside the banking rules only if it meets certain conditions, including belonging to NASASA or a similar approved body, and keeping proper accounting records.

Now, where should the money sit while it waits?

If your stokvel pays out every December, the most important things are safety, and being able to get the money when you need it. Ingram suggests something like a fixed deposit or a money market account, so the money earns interest instead of sitting still.

If your group is saving for more than three years, say for property, he suggests looking at low-cost unit trusts or exchange-traded funds. But only if every member understands that the value can go down as well as up, and the constitution says so.

Let's go back to Thandeka's stokvel. Seventy-two thousand rand, in a stokvel account that needs two of three signatures, with a signed constitution, and a balance read out at every meeting. If the treasurer moves away, falls ill, or simply makes a mistake, the money is still safe, and everyone knows exactly where it is.

And if you're joining someone else's stokvel, ask five questions before you pay anything.

Can I see the constitution? Where is the money kept, and who has to sign before money comes out? Can I see last year's records? What happens if I need to leave early? And when, exactly, do I get paid?

If the answer to any of these is, don't worry, trust us, be careful. And never pay your contribution into someone's personal account.

One more warning. A savings stokvel doesn't make your money grow by itself. If someone promises your stokvel will double your money, or pays old members with new members' money, that's not a stokvel. That's a scheme. Walk away.

Let's recap.

A stokvel is a group of people who save together, by their own rules. More than eleven million South Africans belong to one, saving about fifty billion rand a year.

It works because saving becomes a promise to people you know. But that trust is also the risk: no written rules, money in one person's account, and cash at home.

So protect it. A signed constitution. A separate stokvel bank account. Two of three signatures for every payment. Records everyone can see. And membership of NASASA.
