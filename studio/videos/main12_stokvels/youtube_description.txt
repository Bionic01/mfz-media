More than 11 million South Africans belong to a stokvel, saving about R50 billion a year. It can be one of the best savings habits in the country, or one of the easiest ways to lose money. Follow Thandeka's 12-member stokvel (an illustration: R500 a month each, R72,000 by December) through how stokvels work, why they work, where they go wrong (nothing written down, money in one person's account, cash at home, rotating payouts), and the five steps that protect your money: a signed constitution, a separate stokvel bank account, two of three signatures for every payment, records everyone can see, and NASASA membership. Plus five questions to ask before you join someone else's stokvel.

Key facts
- Ipsos (13 Mar 2024): more than 800,000 stokvel groups, 11 million members, about R50 billion; "cash remains the preferred mode of operation for most stokvels"; mistrust of banks driven by fees, slow account opening and withdrawal rules.
- NASASA (Aug 2025): more than 11 million members; "Young people are joining stokvels in droves."
- Banks Act, Notice 1176 of 2006: a stokvel group stays outside the banking rules only if it meets conditions, including membership of NASASA or a similar approved body and proper accounting records.
- "Stokvel" comes from the English "stock fair" (Dictionary of South African English).

About the example: Thandeka and her stokvel are an illustration. 12 members x R500 a month x 12 months = R72,000; R6,000 each.
